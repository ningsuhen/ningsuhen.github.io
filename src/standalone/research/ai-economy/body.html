Ledger Sankey

The quarterly flow ledger — who paid whom, by quarter, as the engine's stock-flow simulation runs — will be drawn here as a Sankey once the engine publishes its quarterly flows. The deal network above is the point-in-time committed-cash view; the Sankey will be the running accounting view.

"Circular" means round-trip exposure — a measurable flow pattern where committed money returns to its source through a cycle of deals — not an accusation of wrongdoing.

How we know

Every edge is a deal from the fact ledger (the deals table), drawn on its own date: one lane per company, edge colour is the deal type, width is the cash committed (deals with no disclosed amount draw at a thin minimum width and say so in their card). Status is encoded, not just coloured: binding deals are solid, drawn at full opacity with a dot on each lane and an arrowhead at the payee, and are the only deals counted in the committed sums; announced deals are light and tagged, and are excluded from the committed sums; contingent deals — caps and guarantees, not committed cash — are dashed and excluded. “Committed” is used only for binding deals. Lanes are ordered by committed flow; the top 20 are shown and the rest are merged into a single “others (n)” lane so the network stays readable. The highlighted arcs are the round-trip cycles detected in the circularity analysis; the picker isolates one cycle at a time — its edges go to full opacity with a dollar label, everything else drops to 0.12 — and clicking any edge opens its fact card with the source fact linked to the sources section. The ranked list of the largest flows (top 10, each with a status tag) sits under the network at every width. The ledger Sankey needs the engine's quarterly flows, which are not in the bundle yet, so it is a placeholder until the engine publishes them.

Limitations

Limitations: under review.

  1. Re-entry. The model re-enters recession within 4 quarters of an exit 27.1% of the time; history has 1 of 6 (1971–2019). At n = 6 that is not a significant difference (P(≤1 of 6) = 0.48).
  2. Term spread. The model's long-run 10y–3m spread averages 1.39 (90% of paths 0.82–1.94), against 1.69 in history, 1971–2019, so its curve is flatter than history's.
  3. Expansion spell. Mean expansion spell 18.4 quarters vs 24.7 in history, inside the ±2 SE band [8.7, 40.7]. Disclosed in the stationary-moment check.
  4. Safety bounds. The bounds on simulated series sit at least 20% beyond history's extremes; at the worst horizon at most 0.3% of paths touch one (limit 0.5%).
  5. Lab cash. Cash fans include payouts above a cash buffer of about four months of outflows, the big-tech ratio. The upper band (99th percentile $611B for OpenAI, $452B for Anthropic) still exceeds the largest corporate cash pile on record (about $300B), so treat it as illustrative.
  6. Alternative-suite share. The simulated share moving to alternative suites is tiny (2031 median 0.009%), and the model does not yet link it to Adobe or Google revenue.
  7. Unemployment below the historical low. At 60 months, 23.7% of paths run unemployment below the 1971–2019 low (3.12%); the median is 3.87%. Reported, not yet fixed.
  8. HY OAS below the historical minimum. In 0.9% of paths at the worst horizon, the high-yield OAS sits below its historical minimum (155.9 bp). Reported, not yet fixed.
  9. Credit spread above 2,000 bp. At the worst horizon, the model's credit spread exceeds 2,000 bp in 5.1% of paths. Reported, not yet fixed.
  10. Intervene. live — the inlined engine and ModelInput match the bundle's declared build
  11. No backtest yet. The model has not been validated against out-of-sample history; the stationary-moment gate is the closest current check.

2026-10-20

  • How much AI spending leaks abroad now comes from trade data. The model used to assume that 55% of US AI

capital spending buys imports, a figure from 2017, before the AI build-out. It now builds that share from the OECD's input-output tables, grown to 2025 with UN trade data.

  • Computer hardware: 55% imported.
  • Electrical gear: 55%.
  • Cooling equipment: 33%.
  • The building shell: none.
  • These are weighted by what a data centre buys: equipment 60%; the rest split from published construction-cost

breakdowns (electrical about 28%, cooling 23%, shell and fit-out 50%). The result: about 42% of AI spending leaks abroad, so more of the boom counts toward US growth, and its swings move US growth about 30% more than before.

  • Effect on headline odds, on identical random draws: a recession signal within 12 months 23.1% → 22.5%; five-year

odds within 0.4 points.

  • Other regions' exposure is measured, not assumed. How much a region's growth responds to US AI spending was

a judgment. It is now the value that region adds per dollar the US spends, traced through the supply chain: Taiwan's chips inside servers assembled in Mexico count for Taiwan. A region now responds to how far US AI spending departs from the model's central path, measured as the change in that gap over the year.

  • Before, a region reacted only to surprises against each run's own plan. A planned slowdown counted as nothing,

and because the model's feedbacks only ever cut spending, every region carried a small downward bias.

  • Effect: Taiwan and Korea's median growth in 2030 is 2.3%, from 2.0%. Other regions move by 0.1 point or less.
  • Where spending falls 30% or more below the central path, Taiwan and Korea lose 0.2 points of growth in the

worst year at the median, and 1.3 points in the worst 5% of those runs.

  • The IMF forecasts the regions start from are assumed to embed the model's central path. These changes affect

only the regional fans: nothing feeds back from them to the US odds.

  • The credit spread starts where it was observed. The model reads the corporate bond spread (BAA over

Treasuries) off the high-yield spread through a fitted line. On 15 October that line gave 1.66 points against 1.44 observed, so every run started with credit looking worse than it was. Each run now starts at 1.44, and the gap then fades at a rate estimated from three years of daily data. The data can't tell a gap that fades within two quarters from one that persists, so each run draws its rate from the estimate's range. Effect, measured on identical random draws:

  • a recession signal within 6 months: 9.9% → 9.0%;
  • entering an NBER-dated recession within 12 months: 12.7% → 12.2%;
  • five-year odds: 0.3 points or less.
  • The lab cash buffer now comes from big-tech filings. Labs pay nothing out below a cash floor, which was set by

judgment at one year of outflows. Big tech's cash and short-term investments cover between 0.14 years (Amazon) and 0.56 years (Alphabet) of outflows, 0.31 on average, and the floor now uses that.

  • Anthropic's cash at 60 months: median $208B → $112B; 99th percentile $1.1T → $452B.
  • OpenAI's: median $151B → $143B; 99th percentile $632B → $614B.
  • Headline odds did not move.
  • Limitations read from the current check. Three limitations quoted figures from an earlier calibration. They

now come from the current stationary check:

  • the model re-enters recession within 4 quarters of an exit 27% of the time, against 1 of 6 in history;
  • its long-run 10y–3m spread averages 1.39, against 1.69.
  • Current figures. Beyond the changes above, the movement since the previous version is simulation noise from

a re-drawn random sequence, up to 0.3 points.

  • A recession signal within 12 months: 22.5% (long run 26.4%). Within 5 years: 80.4% (80.2%).
  • Entering an NBER-dated recession within 5 years: 62.1%, against 57.2% from a neutral start. The flatter yield

curve adds about 8 points and the AI boom about 4.

  • Under each venue's own rule, the model gives 9.6% against the NY Fed's 12.8%, and 17.9% against Polymarket's

34.5%.

2026-10-19

  • Starting conditions from a single day. The model started from a yield curve 0.2 points steeper than the

observed one, because the 10-year, 3-month and policy rates came from different dates. A steeper curve signals less risk, so near-term recession odds were understated in every earlier version. With all three taken from 15 October 2026:

  • a recession signal within 12 months: 19.8% → 22.9%;
  • entering an NBER-dated recession within 12 months: 8.4% → 12.8%;
  • entering one within 5 years: 59.1% → 62.5%.

The credit-spread starting value still sat 0.2 points above the observed corporate spread, a smaller effect of the same kind. It was fixed on 20 October (above).

  • Lab cash is paid out. Cash above a one-year buffer is now returned to investors at big-tech payout rates

(about 65% of the surplus), and an IPO raise is capped at $30B, just above the largest on record. Before this, lab cash compounded without limit: in 1% of paths Anthropic held $4T by 2031. The 99th percentile is now $1.1T. Headline odds moved by 0.4 points at most.

  • Checked against history. From a neutral start, the simulated economy is compared with twelve long-run

features of US data from 1971 to 2019:

  • the average output gap and unemployment rate;
  • how often recessions happen and how long they and expansions last;
  • how volatile growth is;
  • the chance of a recession starting within five years;
  • the level and spread of corporate credit spreads.

Two fixes followed:

  • unemployment now flattens as the economy runs hot, matching 1968–69 (3.4%) and 2019 (3.6%), where before it

fell in a straight line toward an impossible 2%;

  • credit spreads now move in proportion to their level, and their typical level is set so that the model's

average matches history's in both good times and recessions. Effect on the headline odds was small:

  • a recession signal within 12 months, 18.6% → 19.6%;
  • within 5 years, 80.0% → 78.4%;
  • credit spreads above 6 points within 2 years, 53% → 50%.

Unemployment ends the five years higher than before (4.7% against 4.1% in the baseline run).

  • Today against the long run. Compared like for like with the model's long-run average:
  • the next year is calmer than average (a recession signal within 12 months, 22.5% against 26.4%);
  • five years out it is about average (80.4% against 80.2%).

The high five-year figure mostly reflects how often the "any signal" rule (two negative quarters or a jump in unemployment) fires over five years. Under the identical rule, history gives 74.9% over 1953–2026, so the model runs a few points above history on this measure. The full comparison is on the Macro › Recession tab. On entering an NBER-dated recession within five years, today is 62.1% against 57.2% from a neutral start. What moves it:

  • the flatter-than-usual yield curve adds about 8 points;
  • the AI-investment boom adds about 4;
  • an economy running above potential takes off about 3;
  • a lower natural rate of unemployment than the historical average takes off about 3.

An earlier version of this comparison set two different measures against each other; this one is like for like.

  • Safety bounds. The bounds on simulated series sit at least 20% beyond anything history has recorded, so they

catch numerical errors, not plausible extremes. How much probability the model puts beyond the historical extremes is reported under Limitations.

  • Re-simulate pauses itself whenever the engine built into the page differs from the one that produced the

exported paths, so a re-run is never compared with a baseline from a different model.

  • One interval width. Every range in the report is a 90% interval, from the 5th to the 95th percentile. Fan

charts show 50% and 90% bands.

  • Market comparisons on matching rules only. Each prediction market or survey is set against the model's

probability under that venue's own contract rule, never across rules.

  • NY Fed figure corrected. The yield-curve model's probability was misread from its spreadsheet: 27.4% was an

older row. The probability that the US is in recession in September 2027 is 12.8%; the model's figure under the same rule is 9.9% (6.0% before the starting conditions were corrected).

  • Circular financing counts committed loops only. A loop of deals that returns to where it started is counted

only when every deal in it is binding: 5 loops and $55B. Loops that close only through an announced or contingent deal are listed but not counted.

  • A double count removed. One ledger row recorded a bond's order book ($20B of demand) as money raised. It was

removed, and committed deals now total $1,113.6B.

  • The explosive-price test follows its pre-set rule. It flags a bubble only when the latest month's test

statistic exceeds its 95% critical value. Neither the S&P 500 nor the AI-stock index qualifies today, so the bubble verdict reads 4 of 5 indicators.

  • The engine passed its acceptance tests, and the full run was regenerated: 50,000 paths, with 10,000

exported to this page. Headline odds fell from the earlier draft; a recession within 12 months went from 29% to 19%.
