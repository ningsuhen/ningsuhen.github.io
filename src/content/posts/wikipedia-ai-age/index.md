Recently, I have been thinking about how AI will change or replace many of the existing products on the internet - decentralized office software, open source versions of almost all major proprietary softwares and so on. One of them is how Wikipedia is still like the old simple text based internet. What if AI could make a new version of Wikipedia that is interactive, visual and easy to learn? What if you don't need an expensive book or AI to learn about history or science for free?

So, I created an [idea page](/ideas/wikipedia-ai-age/) for a Wikipedia in the age of AI.

## The idea

Every claim is stored as its own record, linked to the exact passage and version of the source behind it. A named person signs off on what they checked. AI can help find sources, verify quotes and spot gaps, while never signing off itself. Machine-written drafts stay labelled and separate from human-checked text so readers can tell which is which.

## Something I found while checking

<!-- owner:scene This section is titled "Something I found while checking" but gives no reaction. Did anything about the existing guideline surprise you? -->

English Wikipedia already has a content guideline for large language models ([guideline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Writing_articles_with_large_language_models)). In short, it says not to use them to generate or rewrite article text, while allowing translation and suggested fixes to your own writing after review. Since my machine-drafted layer goes beyond that, treat this as a what-if rather than a proposal for how Wikipedia works today.

## What I could not settle

- Whether a model trained on CC BY-SA text produces adapted material. I don't know, and this isn't legal advice.
- Who would run it, how it gets funded, and whether machine drafts should be allowed at all.
- How to measure whether a claim is actually supported by its source, since that comes down to human judgement and I haven't measured it.

The risks table on that page is the part I hope I'm wrong about.
