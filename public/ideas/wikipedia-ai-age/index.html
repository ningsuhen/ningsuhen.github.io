In 30 seconds

  • Wikipedia asks that every fact can be traced to a reliable, published source. In the article, that trace is a footnote.
  • AI now drafts, summarises and reads a lot of text. Some of the sources an editor finds may be generated too.
  • The idea: store each claim as its own record. Each record keeps its sources, the exact passage, the version that was checked, and who or what drafted it.
  • A named person signs what they checked. The AI can find, check and flag. It does not sign.
  • Machine drafts stay labelled, so a reader can tell them from checked text.
  • It is an idea. Nothing is built, and I have no numbers for any of it.

The problem

Wikipedia has a rule behind it that I like. The Verifiability policy says each fact or claim must be attributable to reliable, published sources.

In the article, that rule shows up as a footnote at the end of a sentence. A footnote can name a page or quote a line. As far as I can tell, it does not record in a checkable way which part of the sentence it backs, or which version of the source the editor read. A person has to open the source and compare.

That worked when three things held. I used to take them for granted:

  1. A cited source was written by a person.
  2. A reader who doubted a claim would open the source.
  3. The text was not a copy of other text.

AI makes each one weaker. A source can be generated. A reader can get a paragraph from a chatbot and never see the article, let alone the footnote. Text gets rewritten so often that copies are hard to spot. I do not have a measure of how much of this is happening, so this page gives no number.

Where English Wikipedia stands today

English Wikipedia has a content guideline on large language models. Its short version: do not use them to generate or rewrite article content. It allows two narrow uses. You can have a model suggest fixes to your own writing (spelling, grammar and similar), after you review them. And you can use one for translation, under a separate guidance page. The machine-drafted layer on this page goes further than that. So this is a what-if, not a proposal for how Wikipedia runs now.

Claims, not pages

Think of an essay with references tucked into the paragraphs. Now think of the same facts on index cards, each card with its own source stapled to it. You can check the cards one at a time. You can swap one card without retyping the essay. You can mark one card as disputed and leave the rest alone.

That is the whole shift. The unit is the claim, not the page.

Learn more: what counts as a claim

A claim is one statement with a value, a time and a place. "The dam was finished in 1932" is one claim. Each claim has zero or more support links. A support link points to a source, names the exact part (a page, a table, a timestamp) and says how strong the support is: supports, partly supports, or contradicts. A claim with no support link is marked as unsourced. It is not hidden.

The records

I count seven kinds of record. None of them is ever edited in place. A change adds a new record and keeps the old one.

The seven records

From source to answer

A shop receipt has a till number, and the till has a shift log. If something is wrong, you can walk back along that trail. I want an AI answer to work the same way: from the sentence back to the claim, and from the claim to the source. Provenance just means that trail.

Remove a source

Illustration, not a measurement

One hand-written example with notes I wrote ahead of time. Nothing is computed. It shows the question the idea has to answer: which statement loses its support when one source goes away?

Three claims and three sources. Claim a rests on source A. Claim b rests on source B. Claim c rests on sources A and C.

  • a. The pump station opened in 1931.
  • b. It was built to serve the northern district.
  • c. Its capacity was doubled in 1958.

All three sources are in place. Every claim has support.

The flooded-source loop

Two problems feed each other. The first I call citation laundering: a claim gets a citation, but the chain behind it only repeats the claim and never reaches an original. The second is model collapse. A 2023 paper by Shumailov and colleagues found that training models on model-generated content causes defects that do not go away, and that the rare cases disappear first.

Wikipedia's own policy already guards one door. It says not to cite Wikipedia articles, or sites that mirror Wikipedia, as sources. Generated pages are a wider door. Press Next to walk round the loop.

A claim-level record makes one thing checkable. You can follow a citation chain and see whether it ever reaches an original. It does not tell you whether the original is right. That is still a person's job.

Two layers

The text has two layers, and a reader can always tell which is which. The reviewed layer holds revisions a named person signed, claim by claim. The drafted layer holds machine drafts and anything nobody has checked yet. It is labelled, and it stays out of the reviewed layer until someone signs. Two more states can sit on top: disputed and unsourced.

What a reader sees in each state
StateWho wrote itWhat the reader seesHow it leaves the state
DraftedA model, or a person nobody has reviewed yetA label, not the reviewed markA reviewer signs the revision
ReviewedA person signs after checking the supportThe reviewed mark and the reviewer's nameA dispute, or a newer revision
DisputedAnyone who opens a disputeBoth texts side by side, with the evidenceA resolution revision, or the dispute closes as unresolved
UnsourcedAny revision with no support linkA visible flag on the claimSomeone adds a source and it gets checked

What the AI may do

I would use the AI for jobs a person can check in a minute. It finds, checks, flags and drafts. A named person signs. The AI does not.

Assistant jobs, as I would set them
JobAllowed?Why
Find candidate sources for a claimYes, with reviewA candidate is not a support link until a person links it
Check that a quote matches its sourceYes, with reviewA mismatch gets flagged. A person decides.

Draft a translation of a reviewed claimYes, labelled as a machine draftThe translation gets its own review

Add a source no person has openedNoThat is how invented and laundered citations get in

For comparison, English Wikipedia's current guideline is stricter than this table. It allows copyedit suggestions on your own writing and translation, and nothing else.

Trust signals

A reader needs some sign of how carefully a revision was checked. I can think of four. Each has a weakness I cannot design away.

  • Reviewer record. How many revisions a reviewer signed, and how many were later disputed. Weakness: someone can build a record on easy claims.
  • Signed revisions. A key signs the exact bytes of a revision. Weakness: a signature says who signed. It does not say the claim is true, and keys get stolen.
  • Check date. When the support was last checked against the source. Weakness: a recent date does not mean a careful check.
  • Full history. Every earlier revision is kept and readable. Weakness: history only helps if someone reads it.

Upkeep and money

Stale claims

Each claim carries an "as of" date and a check date. When a source changes or is withdrawn, every claim that rests on it is marked for review. Nothing updates silently.

Other languages

A claim is a statement, not a sentence in one language. The prose around it is written per language. A machine translation stays in the drafted layer until someone who reads that language checks it. Translations can drift, so a changed claim needs a check in every language.

Bots and abuse

Bulk edits are rate limited and held for review. New accounts start with limited reach. Identity checks would cut down fake reviewers, but they cost privacy and shut some people out. I have not picked one. The risk table on the last tab lists the attacks.

Money

Donations, grants and paid use of a model API all carry the same risk: whoever pays can push priorities. I have not picked a funding model. I only want it written down next to the licence, where people can see it.

Staying shared

A commons is something many people use and look after, which no single party can take private. The licence keeps reuse open. Public rules keep the process visible. Neither is enough alone. A licence does not stop one company from running the only reviewer pool.

Explainer, not a design

Each part starts with an everyday picture. "Learn more" has the precise version and the limit. None of these parts is built or tested for this idea.

Content hashes

A fingerprint identifies a person by its exact pattern. Change one ridge and it is a different fingerprint. A content hash does the same for a revision or a source file.

Learn more

A content hash is a cryptographic hash, such as SHA-256, computed over one agreed byte form of a record. The same bytes always give the same hash. A one-byte change gives a different one. If each revision names its parent by hash, the history becomes a chain. You cannot edit an old revision without changing every hash after it.

Limit. A hash proves two copies are the same bytes. It does not prove the bytes are true. Agreeing on the exact bytes to hash is harder than it sounds.

Signed revisions

A wax seal on a letter shows who sealed it, and that nobody opened it since. It says nothing about whether the news inside is true.

Learn more

A reviewer signs a revision's hash with a private key. Anyone with the matching public key can check the signature. A valid signature shows that the key holder signed exactly these bytes. Tying a key to a real person needs a separate system, and I leave that open.

Limit. A valid signature does not show the claim is correct, that the reviewer read the source, or that the key was not stolen. In practice you also need a way to revoke and replace keys.

Citation graph

A road map. Each claim is a town and each support link is a road to a source. You can follow the roads to the original, and you can see when every road loops back into the same few towns.

Learn more

Claims and sources are nodes. Support links are directed edges. A source can cite other sources. Useful questions: which claims rest on this one source; what paths lead from a claim to an original; where the chain loops; which sources have a newer version than the pinned one. A loop is not always wrong. A loop with no original is a warning sign for citation laundering.

Limit. A graph shows links. It does not check that a link supports its claim. That is a person's job.

Answers with ids

A student who answers from a library book also writes down the page numbers. The answer is only as good as those page numbers.

Learn more

In retrieval, a system first finds stored passages (here, claims with their support) and hands them to a model with the question. The answer comes back with the claim ids it used, and the reader can open each one. The answer is a derived text, so it has to say what it draws on.

Limit. A model can say more than it was given, and it can cite a claim that does not say what its sentence says. The check is a person comparing the two, or a second automatic check that has its own errors.

Diffs and disputes

A referee puts two versions side by side, marks the words that differ and attaches the evidence. The decision is written next to it.

Learn more

A claim-level diff compares two revisions of one claim: value, time, place and support links. A page-level diff hides which claim changed. A dispute record holds the two texts, the evidence, a status (open, resolved, withdrawn) and the resolving revision, if there is one. Disputes stay visible after they close.

Limit. A diff shows what changed, not whether the change is right. Long disputes can still stall, so the process needs a way to end.

Provenance standards

A label on a jar says who filled it and when. Anyone can peel it off. A label that is there tells you something. A missing label tells you almost nothing.

Learn more: C2PA and watermarks

C2PA is an open specification for signed provenance data attached to media files. Version 2.2 (May 2025) defines a manifest as the set of provenance information about an asset, built from assertions. It defines a claim as a digitally signed, tamper-evident structure that references those assertions.

Limits the spec states itself. An asset can get separated from its manifest when its metadata is removed or corrupted. The spec says it should not judge whether provenance data is good or bad, and its trust model rests on the identity of the signer.

Watermarks. A watermark is a signal hidden in content. I have not checked how well any watermark survives editing or re-encoding, so I treat it as weak evidence.

For this idea, a provenance standard can say a file came from a signer. It cannot say the claim in the file is true, and it cannot follow text that was copied without its metadata.

Not legal advice

This tab paraphrases the licence and names its sections. It does not say whether any given reuse is allowed. Read the licence itself.

What CC BY-SA asks

Wikipedia text is under Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0. Pages that are not only CC BY-SA text are also under the GFDL. Two parts of the CC licence matter here.

  • Attribution, section 3(a)(1). If you share the material, keep these when the licensor supplied them: who created it, a copyright notice, a notice that points to the licence, a notice that points to the disclaimer of warranties, and a link to the material where that is reasonably practicable. Say if you changed it. Say it is under this licence, with the licence text or a link.
  • ShareAlike, section 3(b). If you share adapted material, the licence you apply must be a Creative Commons licence with the same elements, this version or later, or a BY-SA compatible licence. Include its text or a link. Do not add terms that restrict what that licence allows.

Wikipedia's reuse page says it in its own words. Credit the authors with a link or URL to the page, or a link to a stable free copy, or a list of all authors. Keep a notice that the text is under CC BY-SA, with a link or a copy. Say what you changed.

An attribution line

A made-up example, about a made-up article, to show the parts. It is not a real article.

Example reuse, written ahead of time

"The pump station opened in 1931."

Adapted from "Example Pump Station" (a made-up article) by its contributors, with a link to the page. Licensed under CC BY-SA 4.0, with a link to the licence. Changed: the sentence was shortened.

The parts: who made it (by a link to the page, or the author list), the licence name with a link, and a note of what changed. In the idea, every claim export would carry a line like this.

Questions I cannot answer

  • Is a model trained on CC BY-SA text making adapted material when it writes new text? The licence defines adapted material as derived from or based on the original, changed in a way that needs permission. Whether training fits that, I do not know. The sources I read do not settle it.
  • Does an AI answer that lists claim ids count as sharing those claims? If so, what must it credit? I propose the ids as the credit. That is a design choice, not a legal finding.
  • A link for every claim is more than the licence asks for. Would it make reuse easier, or just noisier? I have not tried it.

Glossary

Open a term to read its meaning. Nothing is stored or sent.

Claim

One statement with a value, a time and a place. The unit a reader checks.

Support link

A link from a claim to a source. It names the exact part of the source and says how strong the support is.

Source

A published work the claim can be checked against, pinned to one version.

Revision

One saved version of a claim. Earlier revisions stay.

Provenance

The trail of where a piece of text came from: which sources, which steps, and who or what drafted and checked it.

Reviewed layer

Revisions a named person signed after checking the support.

Drafted layer

Machine drafts and anything nobody has checked yet. Always labelled.

Dispute

A recorded disagreement about a claim, with the rival text and the evidence. It stays visible after it closes.

Content hash

A fingerprint computed from exact bytes. A one-byte change gives a different fingerprint.

Digital signature

A value made with a private key over exact bytes. It shows who signed those bytes, not that they are true.

Citation laundering

A claim has a citation, but the chain behind it only repeats the claim and never reaches an original.

Model collapse

When models are trained on model output, they get worse in ways that do not go away, and rare cases go first. From a 2023 paper by Shumailov and colleagues.

Retrieval

Finding stored passages and giving them to a model with the question, so the answer can point back to them.

Attribution

The credit a licence asks for: who made it, the notices, a link, and a note of changes.

ShareAlike

The CC BY-SA rule that adapted material is shared under the same or a compatible licence.

C2PA

An open specification for signed provenance data attached to media files.

Sybil reviewer

One person running many reviewer accounts, so they look like many people agreeing.

Commons

Something many people use and look after, which no single party can take private.

Pictures to keep

  • Claims are index cards, not a typed essay. Each card can be checked on its own.
  • Provenance is a receipt trail. You can walk back from the answer to the source.
  • A content hash is a fingerprint. Change one word and the fingerprint changes.
  • A signature is a wax seal. It shows who sealed the letter, not that the news is true.
  • Model collapse is a photocopy of a photocopy. The fine lines go first.

Check yourself

Four questions. Pick an answer and the explanation opens. Nothing is stored.

1. Why keep sources per claim instead of per page?

Answer

The first. A support link per claim shows which part of which source backs which statement. A list of footnotes at page level does not.

2. A reviewer's signature checks out. What does that prove?

Answer

The second. A signature covers the bytes. It says nothing about truth, or about who typed the text.

3. A generated page cites a second generated page, which cites nothing. What is this?

Answer

The second. Each link looks like a source, but the chain never reaches an original.

4. Which of these is on the CC BY-SA 4.0 list of things to keep when you share?

Answer

The third. Section 3(a)(1) lists a copyright notice among the items to keep, when the licensor supplied one. Payment and reader details are not in it.

How answers work

Answers come from short notes written from this page. There is no AI model. Your question is matched against the notes by keyword (a ranking called BM25), right here in the page. If nothing matches well, it says it does not know. Every answer links to the section it came from. Nothing you type is stored or sent, and the conversation is gone when you leave the page.

Typed questions only. There is no microphone and no spoken reply.

Open problems

  • How to measure whether a claim is supported. A support link is a human judgement, and I have not measured how often two reviewers agree.
  • How to tell a generated source from a human one at scale. I do not know a reliable way.
  • How to keep reviewing fair without public identities that put people at risk.
  • How many languages a small review pool can cover, and what happens to the rest.
  • How to let readers ask questions without the answer replacing the claims.
  • Whether claim-level records are worth the cost for topics that change slowly.

Not decided

I left these open on purpose. Any of them could change the design.

  • Who runs it, and under what rules.
  • How it is paid for.
  • Whether machine drafts are allowed at all, or only for translation.
  • Which licence new text would use, and whether it matches Wikipedia's.
  • Whether any code gets written. This page is the whole output so far.
