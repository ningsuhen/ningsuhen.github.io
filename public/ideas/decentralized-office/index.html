In 30 seconds

  • Today a hosted office suite keeps your documents, mail and calendar as readable data on the provider's servers.
  • The idea: the data stays on your devices. Every document gets its own key. The keys go only to the people who should read it.
  • The cloud keeps encrypted backups it cannot open. A relay passes encrypted changes it cannot open. Devices merge changes themselves, even after working offline.
  • It is an idea. Nothing here is built or tested, and the crypto is only the general kind used in other systems.

The problem with a central workspace

A central workspace works because one company holds everything. That same fact is the problem. The provider can read every document, index it, scan it, hand it to a court or lose it in a breach. An administrator with the right role can open any file. Someone who takes over an account can read all of it.

Most people accept this because the alternative looked hard. Collaboration seemed to need a server that sees the text, so that it can merge edits and run search. That assumption is what this idea tries to remove.

Enterprises feel it most. Their contracts, pricing sheets and HR files sit in a system that a third party can read. Privacy-minded people feel it too, and they often give up the tools they like because the trade is so one-sided.

The idea in one picture

A change starts on one laptop, is sealed there, travels through a relay that cannot open it, and is opened and merged on another laptop. The backup is sealed too. Press Next to follow one change through the picture.

Principles

  1. The data lives where the people work. Each device keeps a full local copy, so reading and editing do not need a server.
  2. Servers move and store ciphertext. A relay, a sync host or a backup provider should be unable to read content, even if it wants to.
  3. Each document has its own key. Access is a copy of that key, wrapped for each person who may read the document.
  4. Changes are signed. A receiver checks the author before it applies a change.
  5. Merging does not need a referee. Replicas converge on their own when they exchange changes (see Sync).
  6. Removing someone means new keys. Revoking a permission is not enough once a person has copied the key. The document must be rekeyed.
  7. Say what leaks. Sizes, timing and who-talks-to-whom are hard to hide. The page lists them instead of hiding them.

The parts

Local-first node
The app on a person's device. It holds the full working copy of every document the person can open, and it works without a network.
Encrypted store
The local database. Document content is stored sealed under the document key, so a stolen disk does not give plain text by itself. Protecting the disk and the keys in memory is a separate job.
Identity and keys
Each person has a key pair for receiving keys and a key pair for signing changes. The private halves never leave the devices they were made on.
Sharing and groups
A group is a list of members and the public keys of their devices. A signed group change adds or removes members.
Sync relay
A server that forwards sealed changes between devices. It can route, queue and store them, but it holds no keys for the content.
Encrypted backup
A cloud store for sealed snapshots. A recovery key, kept by the person, is what lets a new device open them.

Keys and sharing

The key structure has three layers. A person's identity keys open the copies of the document key that were addressed to them. The document key opens the content. When the group changes, the document key is replaced and wrapped again for the members who remain.

Honest limit

Rekeying a large group takes work for every remaining member. A real design would have to choose how often to rekey and how to handle devices that are offline when it happens.

Offline edits and merge

Two people edit the same document while they are apart. Neither copy is the master. Each device keeps its own state. Changes are recorded as operations, and the merge rule is built so that the result does not depend on the order the operations arrive in.

This is the job of a conflict-free replicated data type, or CRDT. For a list of lines, a common approach gives every inserted line a unique id and records which line it was inserted after. Deletes leave a marker instead of removing the line. With those rules, two replicas that have seen the same set of operations show the same list.

Not every kind of document merges this cleanly. Free-text paragraphs, spreadsheet formulas and rich formatting each need their own rules. Some combinations give results that a person would call wrong, even when the replicas agree. That is one of the open problems on the Trade-offs tab.

Signed operations

Every operation is signed by its author's signing key before it is sealed. A receiver checks the signature against the member list it already trusts. An operation that fails the check is dropped. This stops a relay from inventing edits, and it stops a removed member from writing into the new epoch with an old key.

Signing does not hide anything. The author is still visible to the members who open the operation. It proves who made a change. It does not prove that the change was wise.

Backup and recovery

A cloud copy protects against a lost laptop. It should not be able to read the copy. The snapshot is sealed on the device before it is uploaded, under a backup key. That backup key comes from a recovery key the person keeps, offline, in a place they choose.

The trade is plain. If the recovery key is lost and no device still holds the keys, the backup cannot be opened. That is the price of the cloud not being able to read it.

Losing a device

Two cases are different. A locked, encrypted laptop that is lost is a bad day, but its content is protected by the disk and device keys. A laptop that is lost while it is unlocked, or while its keys sit in memory, is a worse case. Until the rest of the group revokes that device and rekeys the documents, whoever has the laptop can read what it holds.

The design has to say what a remote revoke can and cannot do. It can stop the device from receiving new keys and new changes. It cannot take back a copy that was already read.

Metadata

Encryption hides content, not everything else. A relay still sees that two devices talk, how often, and how big the sealed blobs are. Group size and rekey timing can hint at how a team is organised. Padding and batching reduce some of this. They cost time and bandwidth, and they do not remove it.

Stated plainly

The design makes the content private. It does not make the activity invisible to whoever runs the relay.

Glossary

Tap a term to open its definition. Nothing is stored, and nothing is sent anywhere.

Ciphertext

Data after encryption. Without the key it looks like random bytes. Ciphertext can still reveal its length.

End-to-end encryption

Only the two ends hold keys, so a server in between passes data it cannot read. The design works only if keys never reach the server.

Symmetric key

One secret key that both encrypts and decrypts. AES-256 is the common choice. Two parties must already share the key.

Key pair

A public key you can give out and a private key you keep. Used to receive keys or check signatures without sharing the secret.

Key agreement

Two parties combine their own private key with the other's public key and get the same shared secret, without sending it. ECDH is the common method.

Key wrapping

Encrypting one key under another key, so the wrapped key can be stored or sent. The document key is wrapped once for each member.

HKDF

A standard way to turn a shared secret into one or more keys of the right form. Defined in RFC 5869.

Digital signature

A value only the key holder can make, that anyone with the public key can check. It shows who made a change and that the change was not altered.

CRDT

A replicated data type whose merge rule gives the same result for any order of the same operations. Replicas can edit alone and converge later.

Epoch

A period in which one set of keys is in use for a group. A removal or addition starts a new epoch.

Forward secrecy

Old keys cannot be used to read new messages, because the key that protects the newer messages was derived from something that has since been thrown away.

Metadata

Data about the data: who talks to whom, when, how large a blob is, and who is in a group. Often visible even when content is sealed.

Relay

A server that stores and forwards messages between devices. In this design it handles ciphertext only.

Bridge

A gateway between this design and classic email. At the bridge, a message is in plain text or re-sealed, so the bridge is a trusted hop.

Recovery key

A secret the person keeps, used to open a backup. If it is lost and no device holds the keys, the backup cannot be opened.

The techniques, with analogies

Each technique starts with an everyday picture. Open "Learn more" for the precise version.

End-to-end encryption

Like a locked box that only the two people at either end have keys to. The courier carries the box and cannot open it.

Learn more

Keys exist only on the endpoints. Servers store and forward sealed data. Content is protected. Routing, timing and size are not.

Per-document keys and key wrapping

Like each folder having its own padlock. The key to each padlock goes in a small envelope addressed to each person who may open it.

Learn more

Each document has a random 256-bit key. That key is encrypted separately for each member, using the member's public key or a key derived from it. Changing the key later means wrapping a new copy for each member who stays.

Key pairs and key agreement

Like a mail slot anyone can drop into, where only you have the key to the box behind it. Someone can send to you without meeting you first.

Learn more

A public key encrypts or agrees a secret, and the private key reverses it. ECDH with a derived key, through HKDF, is a common way to get a shared secret for wrapping. The protocol X3DH, in the Signal specifications, uses this for a first message sent before the recipient is online.

Signatures

Like a signature on a letter. It shows who wrote it, and a changed word breaks it.

Learn more

A signature is made with a private signing key and checked with the public one. Signing every operation lets receivers reject forged or altered changes, including ones that a relay injects.

CRDTs

Like two people writing in two notebooks on a train. When they meet, the same rule merges the pages, whichever notebook is read first.

Learn more

A CRDT is a data type where the merge function is commutative, associative and idempotent. Replicas can apply the same operations in any order and reach the same state. Lists of lines, counters and sets are common examples. Rich text is harder, and it is an open problem here.

Epochs and forward secrecy

Like changing the locks after a key goes missing. The new locks are for the people who still have a right to the house.

Learn more

The Messaging Layer Security protocol, RFC 9420, is a standard for group key agreement. Members advance through epochs as they are added or removed, and each epoch has its own secrets. That is close to what this design needs for group keys. The design does not choose MLS yet.

Metadata

Like the outside of an envelope. It shows the sender, the postmark and the weight, not the letter inside.

Learn more

Metadata includes blob sizes, timing, who syncs with whom and group membership. Hiding it costs padding, batching and cover traffic. Each option has a bandwidth or latency cost.

Search over encrypted data

Like keeping a card index at home. The post office cannot search it, so you search it yourself.

Learn more

The simple answer is a full-text index built on each device from decrypted content, and never sent anywhere. Searching across a group, or on a server, needs searchable encryption or blind indexes. Those leak which items match which searches, so they trade privacy for convenience.

Email and interop with SMTP

Like a sealed letter inside a postcard. The postcard can be read by everyone who handles it.

Learn more

Classic email carries the envelope in the clear: sender, recipient, subject and the route. OpenPGP (RFC 9580, 2024) and S/MIME are existing ways to seal the body. A bridge to classic email either decrypts the message or re-seals it, so the bridge is where plain text exists. The design must say that the bridge is trusted.

Enterprise admin and compliance without plaintext access

Like a building manager who can open the doors but cannot look inside the rooms. They can manage who has keys, but not what is in the files.

Learn more

Admins manage membership and policy through signed group changes. Legal discovery and retention need access to content. Ways to meet that include keeping keys with the organisation, or with a designated custodian, or a holder who can export. Each choice gives up some of the privacy the design promises. That tension is stated on the Trade-offs tab.

Key recovery and revocation

Like a spare key with a trusted neighbour, or a note in a safe. Both help when you lose your keys. Both are only as safe as the place you keep them.

Learn more

Recovery uses a recovery key, a split key, or a set of trusted devices or people. Revocation adds a new epoch and rewraps the document key for the remaining members. It cannot undo a copy that was already read.

Check yourself

Four questions. Pick an answer. The explanation opens after you pick, or open it at any time.

1. A backup provider is breached. What can the attacker read?

Answer

The second. The provider holds sealed blobs. The backup key comes from the recovery key, which the person keeps. A breach gives ciphertext and sizes, not content.

2. A member is removed. Can they read edits made after the removal?

Answer

The second. The removed member keeps what they copied before. A new epoch with a new key is wrapped only for the remaining members, so later edits are sealed under a key they do not hold.

3. Two people edit the same document offline. Why do the copies agree after they sync?

Answer

The second. That is the point of a CRDT. The server does not decide the result, and the order of arrival does not matter.

4. Does sealing the content also hide who talks to whom?

Answer

The second. Encryption protects content. Metadata is a separate problem with separate costs to reduce.

Open problems

  • Search. Full text across a group needs an index on each device, or leaks match patterns on a server.
  • Onboarding. A new device must get keys without a central directory that can lie about who it is.
  • Key loss. A lost recovery key with no surviving device means lost data. Any recovery scheme moves the risk somewhere else.
  • Mobile. Background sync, battery use and OS limits on keys make a phone harder than a laptop.
  • Spam. A server cannot filter sealed mail by content. Filtering has to happen on the device, or use metadata.
  • Legal discovery. Courts and regulators may want content the organisation cannot read. That pulls against the design.
  • Rich documents. Spreadsheet formulas, formatting and comments are harder to merge than lines of text.
  • Growth. Merge histories and tombstones grow. Compaction must not break convergence.

What is not decided

  • Which group-key protocol to use, or whether to build on MLS.
  • Where a relay runs, who runs it, and whether a person can run their own.
  • How identities are proved to other people.
  • The default recovery policy, and how recovery is offered to people who will not manage a key.
  • Which office features are in scope first, and how each one merges.
  • How legal and compliance access is handled.
  • Pricing, hosting and any business model.
