Question and verdict

The question

A creator with a voice-synthesis API key wants technical videos with their own talking head, slide-style explainers and some AI footage. Which tools, at what quality and cost?

Short answer

Film the talking head, clone the voice for fixes, build slides and diagrams as code, and buy AI B-roll by the second. Iterate in a cheap draft tier and render the final once. Use an avatar only where filming is not possible.

Key findings

  • Avatar plans start near $25 to $30 a month, but most hide the cost per minute

    HeyGen Creator is $29 a month ($24 billed yearly), Synthesia Starter $29, Captions Max $24.99. HeyGen's own credit guide gives no credits per minute; the cost shows in the app before you generate. Hedra is the exception: its API lists avatar and lip-sync from 2.5 cents a second, about $1.50 a minute.

    Sources: [1] [2] [3] [7] [5]

  • ElevenLabs has no avatar model of its own

    Its Image and Video product (beta) runs third-party models, including lip-sync tools such as HeyGen Avatar 4 and OmniHuman 1.5. Voice cloning: Instant from Starter ($6 a month), Professional from Creator ($22 a month).

    Sources: [8] [10] [11]

  • Sora is gone, and the Veo 3.1 preview on the Gemini API just closed

    OpenAI's deprecations page lists the Videos API and the sora-2 models as removed on 2026-09-24. The Veo 3.1 preview models on the Gemini API were shut down on 2026-10-22. Veo 3.1 is still sold through Runway's API at $0.40 a second with audio and $0.20 without.

    Sources: [22] [14] [16]

  • AI B-roll ranges from under 2 cents to 40 cents a second

    Alibaba's wan2.2-i2v-flash costs $0.015 a second at 480P; MiniMax H3 $0.08 at 768P; Runway Gen-4.5 $0.12; Veo 3.1 with audio $0.40. With three tries per kept clip, a finished minute of B-roll costs roughly $3 to $72.

    Sources: [20] [19] [16]

  • A draft tier pays for itself within two review rounds

    Luma's Draft mode costs about 5% of its 1080p rate; Veo 3.1 Fast and Gen-4 Turbo cost 42 to 50% of their final pairs. Drafting B-roll and re-rendering only the chosen shots breaks even at 2 rounds or fewer in every pair modelled. Tiering the voice (Kokoro, then ElevenLabs) saves time, not money. See Draft and final.

    Sources: [17] [16] [9]

  • YouTube: cloning your own voice needs no label; realistic synthetic people and places do

    YouTube lists "cloning one's own voice to create voice overs" as a minor change. Content that makes a real person appear to say or do something they did not, or shows a realistic scene that did not happen, must be disclosed. Mass-produced, templated videos with little variation are not monetised.

    Sources: [36] [37]

Three ways to get a face

  1. Film yourself. A real likeness at no marginal cost if a camera or phone is already owned. OBS Studio is free (GPL v2 or later) [35]. Mistakes can be fixed in the edit with a cloned-voice patch over B-roll or a slide.
  2. Avatar clone of yourself. Record training footage once; afterwards type a script and get video. HeyGen, Synthesia, Colossyan, Captions and D-ID sell this as a subscription; Hedra sells it per second through an API.
  3. Lip-sync over real footage. Keep a filmed take and re-time the mouth to new audio. Open-source models (LatentSync, MuseTalk) and ElevenLabs' third-party lip-sync tools do this. It suits short fixes better than whole videos (judgment).

ElevenLabs and video

ElevenLabs sells voices, not faces. Its Image and Video product, in beta, is a front end to other companies' models: Veo 3.1, Kling, Wan and Seedance for video, and lip-sync tools including OmniHuman 1.5 (not available in the US), HeyGen Avatar 4, Veed Fabric, Creatify Aurora and Sync [10] [11]. The free plan covers images only; per-generation credit costs are shown in the app, not published.

For a creator who already pays for ElevenLabs, this is a convenient way to try several lip-sync models with one bill. It does not add an ElevenLabs-made avatar.

Fit for technical content

Judgment, not measurement

Technical videos keep the talking head on screen for a fraction of the runtime; most of the screen time goes to code, diagrams and demos. That lowers the value of an avatar: a few minutes of real footage per video covers the face, and an avatar's subscription buys a likeness the viewer sees only briefly. An avatar earns its cost when filming is the bottleneck: frequent short updates, translations, or a creator who will not appear on camera.

Slides plus TTS

A simple automated flow: write the script per slide, synthesise one audio file per slide, export each slide as an image, then let ffmpeg hold each image for its audio's length and join the parts. Remotion or Motion Canvas replace the image step when slides need motion. The voice cost is small: about 9,000 characters for 10 minutes of narration (1,500 words at 150 words a minute; an estimate), or about $0.72 at ElevenLabs' $0.08 per 1,000 characters API rate [9].

Monetisation risk

YouTube's inauthentic-content policy names image slideshows or scrolling text with little narrative or educational value, and templated content with minimal variation, as not monetisable [37]. Slide videos with real explanation are a different thing, but a fully automated slides-plus-TTS channel sits close to that line.

  • OpenAI Sora: shutdown announced 2026-03-24; the Videos API and the sora-2 and sora-2-pro models were removed on 2026-09-24 [22]. OpenAI's help article (seen only through search, the page refused a direct fetch) gives 2026-04-26 for the web and app.

Captions

YouTube generates captions automatically with speech recognition, with accuracy that varies [47]. Technical terms are where automatic captions tend to fail (judgment), so uploading a caption file is worth the small cost. Whisper (MIT) and whisper.cpp (MIT) run locally for free [48]. OpenAI's API lists whisper-1 at $0.006 a minute, about $0.06 for a 10-minute video, and gpt-4o-mini-transcribe at an estimated $0.003 a minute [43]. When the narration is synthesised from a script, the script itself is the caption text; only the timing is needed.

Cheapest

$0 to $10 per video

Camera, local tools, open weights

Film yourself with OBS. Slides in Marp or Slidev, animation in Manim, joined with ffmpeg. Narration from Kokoro-82M locally (stock voice) or ElevenLabs Starter for an Instant clone. $60 to $75 per video

Camera first, paid B-roll seconds, one voice subscription

Top quality

$122 to $230 per video

Camera, premium B-roll, transcript-based editing

Film yourself. Remotion for templated, data-driven scenes with Manim inserts. ElevenLabs Creator (or Pro) Professional clone. B-roll from Veo 3.1 (with or without audio) and Runway Gen-4.5 or Aleph for edits. Descript Creator for transcript editing.

Avatar instead of camera

A subscription avatar replaces the camera line with a share of the plan: HeyGen Creator is $29 / 4 = $7.25 a video, Synthesia Pro $89 / 4 = $22.25. Whether 40 minutes of avatar video a month fit inside those plans could not be checked: HeyGen publishes no credits per minute, and Synthesia Pro includes about 70 minutes, Starter about 22.

What moves the total

  • B-roll seconds and retries. At Veo 3.1 Fast prices each extra on-screen minute adds $18 at 3 tries; halving the retry factor saves more than dropping every subscription.
  • Audio on B-roll. Veo 3.1 doubles in price with audio. B-roll under narration usually does not need its own sound (judgment).
  • Videos per month. Subscriptions are a small share; at one video a month the best-value total rises by about $16.50.
  • Avatar minutes. If the avatar covers only the 2 to 3 minutes the face is actually on screen, Hedra's cost falls to $3 to $4.50.

Keeping tiers in sync

  1. One shot list. A single file holds, per shot: id, script text, start and end time, track (voice, B-roll, code, face), prompt, seed, model, keyframe image. Both tiers read it; nothing is edited in the timeline alone.
  2. Audio first. Narration sets the clock. Draft audio gives draft timings; after the final voice is rendered, re-time the shot list from its timestamps instead of forcing the final voice to the draft lengths.
  3. Timings from the audio. ElevenLabs returns character start and end times with the audio [51]. Kokoro and Piper do not document timing output, so draft timings need a forced-alignment or transcription step.
  4. Stitching. When the final voice is rendered per shot, ElevenLabs' previous and next text or request-id fields carry prosody across the joins [51].
  5. Pinned generation settings. Prompt, seed, keyframe and model version per shot, recorded in the shot list, so a rejected final can be retried from the same inputs.
  6. Code scenes read the same timings. Remotion or Manim scenes take durations from the shot list, so they re-time without manual edits.

What to recheck after the final render

  • Pacing: total length and every cut point, since the final voice will not match draft timings exactly.
  • Voice: prosody at joins, pronunciation of technical terms, and the seam between filmed audio and cloned patches.
  • Lip-sync: any avatar or lip-synced segment has to be regenerated against the final audio and watched again.
  • B-roll: the final render of a shot can differ from its draft; check content, on-screen text and anything that must be accurate.
  • Disclosure: the AI-use setting still follows the final content.

Cost and break-even

B-roll dominates, so the model covers B-roll. Each review round regenerates the full 180 on-screen seconds at 3 tries per kept second (540 seconds). All-final renders every round at the final price. Two-tier renders the rounds at the draft price, then the chosen 180 seconds once at the final price, with 1.5 tries per shot if the draft carries over well, or 3 if it does not.

  • Above two review rounds, two tiers are cheaper in every pair

    Even when the draft carries over poorly, break-even is at 2 rounds or fewer. With good carry-over it breaks even at the first round or sooner. The cheaper the draft relative to the final, the faster the gap grows: at 5 rounds the Luma pair costs about a sixth of rendering everything at 1080p.

  • The voice tier saves time, not money

    All-final voice costs about $0.72 per round; two-tier saves $0.72 for every round after the first. Tier the voice for speed and freedom to rewrite, not for cost.

What was not tested

Read this first

No video, avatar or voice was generated for this report. Visual quality, lip-sync accuracy, likeness, artefacts on code or text in frame, and generation speed of the paid services are all unmeasured. Every quality statement on this page is either a vendor's own claim or labelled as judgment.
  • The retry factor of 3 is an assumption. Real rates depend on the model, the prompt and how picky the editor is.
  • How well a draft carries over to its final render (the 1.5 against 3 tries in the Draft and final tab) is an assumption; it was not measured for any model.
  • Open-weights speeds are README figures for a given GPU, not reruns.
  • Prices were read through an automated page reader; a quick look at the live page before buying is worth it.

Pitfalls

  • Credits hide the real price. HeyGen, Captions and ElevenLabs Image and Video show the cost per generation only in the app. Run one test video and read the credit counter before choosing a plan.
  • Introductory prices. ElevenLabs Starter and Creator show $1 and $11 first months; the cost model uses the regular price.
  • Model churn. Sora is gone and the Veo 3.1 Gemini API preview closed three days before this report. A pipeline that calls one model by name needs a fallback.
  • Licence fine print. HunyuanVideo's licence excludes the EU, UK and South Korea. LTX-2.5 and later need a paid licence for entities with $10M or more in annual revenue. Remotion needs a company licence from 4 people up. D-ID's Trial and Lite plans are personal use only.
  • Disclosure. Realistic AI footage of real places needs the "AI use" label; an avatar of the creator most likely does too.
  • Templated channels. Fully automated slides-plus-TTS videos with little variation risk the inauthentic-content rule.

Not verified

These could not be confirmed on an official page and are not used in the cost model:

  • Kling: every plan and API price (pages rendered no content). The figure seen in search results, about $7 a month, is not confirmed.
  • D-ID: all plan and API prices, including the "from about $6" seen in search results.
  • Veo 3.1 per-second prices from Google itself (Vertex pricing did not render; Gemini API pricing has no Veo rows). Runway's resale prices are used instead.
  • ByteDance's own Seedance token prices (BytePlus table did not render); MiniMax Hailuo consumer plans; Google AI Ultra price; Flow credits per Veo clip.
  • HeyGen credits per avatar minute and API per-minute rates; Synthesia Starter yearly price; Hedra subscription plans; Colossyan monthly price.
  • Whether Wan 2.5 and later have open weights; LTX-2 minimum GPU memory.

How to test before buying

  1. Write one 60-second technical script with jargon, numbers and a code term.
  2. Render it with two avatar services on their free or entry plans, and with your own camera.
  3. Patch one sentence with an Instant and a Professional voice clone; listen for the seam.
  4. Generate the same three B-roll prompts on two models; count tries to a usable clip. That count replaces the retry factor of 3.
  5. Record the credits each step used. That gives the real cost per finished minute for the cost model.
